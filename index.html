Where do hill villages depend on springs, and who is most at risk?

This atlas maps 1,262 Nagaland villages by how likely they are to rely on springs for water, who needs support most, and where new built-up land could press on spring areas. It is an open-data research prototype made for academic purposes.

How to read this map

Model score is the model's estimate (0 to 1) that a village reports a spring as its water source in Census 2011. Villages at 0.58 or more are called high reliance (top third).

Equity score is the average of three ranks: model score, Scheduled Tribe share, and no tap or handpump. Higher means greater need.

  • Restore: high reliance and below-median tree cover
  • Regulate: high reliance and fast built-up growth
  • Protect: other high reliance villages
  • Guide growth: fast growth, lower reliance
  • Monitor: everything else

Growth scenarios are what-if runs of new built-up area from 2020 to 2030, not forecasts. Rapid is 1.5 times the trend. Managed keeps the trend total but steers it away from high reliance villages.

Drag to move, scroll or pinch to zoom, click a village, a village area or a district. With the map focused, arrow keys move it and + and − zoom.

About this project

Springs and springsheds

A spring is a place where groundwater reaches the surface on its own, so people can use the water without pumping [1]. A springshed is the whole system that feeds a spring: the aquifer under the ground, and the land and watershed above it where rain soaks in [1]. Looking after a spring therefore means looking after its recharge land, and not only the spot where the water comes out.

Why springs matter in the hills

In the hills, springs often take the place of rivers, wells and pipelines. The NITI Aayog working group on Himalayan springs gives some rough national figures [1]:

PointWhat the report says
Number of springsAbout 3 million springs lie in the Indian Himalayan Region, out of a rough estimate of 5 million for India.
People who depend on themThe region has nearly 50 million residents, and about 60% of them are estimated to depend on spring water.
NagalandIts village table lists spring use in 639 of 1,428 villages (44.7%). The report says this table is only representative and probably understates real use.
DeclineNearly half of the perennial springs in the region are reported to have dried up or become seasonal.
Causes namedChanges in rainfall and temperature, rising demand for water, land use change including roads and buildings, and drilling that competes with springs for the same aquifers.
What it recommendsA proper inventory of springs and springshed management, which means protecting and recharging the land that feeds each spring. It also notes pilot springshed work in Mokokchung and Tuensang districts of Nagaland.

These are figures from the report and are not results of this atlas. This atlas uses a different list of 1,262 villages from the Census, so its counts do not match the report's table.

What "spring reliance" means here

A village counts as spring-reliant in this atlas when the Census 2011 village directory lists a spring among its water sources [2]. This is a stand-in. It says that a village reported a spring, and it does not say where the spring is, how much water it gives or how many households use it. It is used because Nagaland has no open map of spring points, and the Census is the only source that treats every village in the same way.

Since the springs themselves cannot be seen, the model learns what the surroundings look like in villages that report springs (terrain, soil, rainfall and tree cover) and gives every village a score for how much it resembles them. A high score means the surroundings look like places that report springs. It is a hint on where to look first and it is not proof that a spring exists.

What this academic project asks

The work was made for academic purposes, to test whether open data can support planning around springs where no spring map exists. It asks four questions:

  1. Where? Which villages are likely to depend on springs.
  2. Who? Which of those villages have the greatest need. Need is judged from Scheduled Tribe share and from having no tap or handpump, so that support can reach the villages with fewer alternatives.
  3. What threatens them? Where new built-up land could press on the land that feeds springs, tested under three growth scenarios.
  4. What is missing? Local and indigenous knowledge of springs, such as where they are, how they have changed and how villages look after them. This is not yet in the atlas and would need field work and interviews.

The aim is springshed-based planning, which means planning around the land that feeds water sources and not only around village or district boundaries. Real springshed boundaries are not available, so this atlas works at village level and should be seen as a first screening step.

How it was built

  1. Target. For each of 1,262 villages, whether Census 2011 lists a spring as a water source [2]. 600 villages do and 662 do not, so a plain guess of "no spring" would be right 52.5% of the time. Any model has to beat that.
  2. Predictors. 21 village-level variables on terrain, rainfall, soil and land cover, linked to village boundaries through the SHRUG platform [3].
  3. Models. Random forest, gradient boosting and logistic regression, built in KNIME.
  4. Testing. Each model was tested in three ways: on random folds, on 10 km spatial blocks, and by leaving one whole district out. The harder tests are the honest ones, because nearby villages are alike.
  5. Scores and zones. The model score, the equity score and the planning zones were then calculated for every village.
  6. Growth. Built-up land in 2000, 2010 and 2020 was measured inside each village area from the Global Human Settlement Layer [4] to set the trend and build the scenarios.

The most used variables in the tree models were terrain elevation, relief, soil sand content, tree cover, drainage flow and rainfall. These counts show what the model used, not what causes springs.

An area under the curve (AUC) of 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect. The models are modestly useful on nearby unseen land and weak on unseen districts, where they do no better than a simple overlay score (0.572). So the map is best read as a screening aid, and the district-level results should be treated with care.

Known limits and possible errors

This is a learning project. It was built by a student while learning the tools, and it has not been checked in the field or by an outside reviewer. Some figures, labels or map details may be wrong.
  • Old data. Census 2011 is more than a decade old. Villages and water sources have changed since.
  • A stand-in, not a spring map. The target says a village lists a spring as a water source. It does not show where a spring is, whether it flows all year or how much water it gives.
  • Towns are missing. The village table has no towns, so urban areas such as Dimapur and Kohima are under-represented in the figures.
  • Districts have changed. Nagaland has been divided into more districts since 2011. This atlas uses the 11 districts of the 2011 Census.
  • Boundaries do not match exactly. District lines come from a different source than village positions, so some villages sit on the wrong side of a line near district edges. Village areas are simplified to keep the page fast.
  • Headquarters are approximate. The district headquarter markers are placed by hand.
  • Local knowledge is not included. See the fourth question above.
  • Weak transfer to new districts. See the test table above.
  • Scenarios are not forecasts. They are simple what-if runs, not predictions of where building will happen.
  • Elevation and tree cover maps. The whole-region layers come from SRTM [7] and ESA WorldCover [8], processed in Google Earth Engine [9], and are coarser than the originals. They can differ from the village-level terrain and land cover values used by the model, which come from a different source.
  • Context map. Neighbouring states, rivers and towns come from Natural Earth [6] and are for orientation only. Borders shown are not an authority on any boundary.

Please do not use this atlas to site wells or to decide funding without field checks. If you spot an error, please tell the project team so it can be corrected.

About the project

This atlas was made for academic purposes, as a learning and research project in planning, using open data only. The data work was done in KNIME, and the website was built to share the results in an easy, visual way.
